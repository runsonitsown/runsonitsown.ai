# Anti-AI Writing Patterns — TJ Beatty Brand
# Master Reference for All Writing Skills

Load this file before executing any skill that produces copy for the RunsOnItsOwn.ai brand.

---

## OVERVIEW

These patterns are forbidden across all TJ Beatty-branded writing because they signal AI-generated content. They violate the authenticity principle that anchors the brand.

---

## Pattern 1: Anaphora (Constructed Rhetorical Repetition)

**What it is:** Starting consecutive sentences with the same word as a stylistic device.

**Examples to avoid:**
- "Every time you go vacant. Every time you shut down. Every time you repeat the same pattern."
- "You can change it. You can improve it. You can rise up."
- "That's fear. That's the wall you don't know you're building. That's the distance you're creating."

**Why it signals AI:** Rhetorical repetition feels engineered, not conversational. It's a writing technique, not how humans think or speak naturally.

**Exception — Natural subject continuity is acceptable:**
- Good: "You bought something. Something your soul has been asking for." (Different grammatical subjects; flows naturally)
- Good: "What creates this pattern? A belief that has been reinforced for decades." (Question answered directly; not constructed repetition)
- Good: "He was angry. Angry enough to act." (Not stylistic repetition; natural emotional intensification)

## Pattern 2: Em Dashes

**What it is:** Using — to connect clauses, create pauses, or insert asides.

**Examples to avoid:**
- "This is the real issue — the one nobody talks about."
- "You're stuck — not because you're broken, but because nobody taught you."
- "That's where TJ comes in — with a framework that actually works."

**Why it signals AI:** Em dashes are a signature AI habit. Humans rewrite the sentence or use a period instead.

**No exceptions:** Em dashes are forbidden without exception across all TJ brand copy. Rewrite using:
- A period (strongest break)
- A comma (lighter pause)
- Restructuring the sentence entirely

**Example rewrites:**
- Bad: "This is where I come in — with a framework that actually works."
- Good: "This is where I comes in. My framework actually works."
- Good: "My framework is the difference."

## Pattern 3: Vague Hook Questions

**What it is:** Emotional rhetorical questions that sound intimate but convey no concrete information.

**Examples to avoid:**
- "The person who wanted to get closer?"
- "The result?"
- "Those patterns you've been struggling with?"
- "The one pattern that keeps showing up?"

**Why it signals AI:** Creates false intimacy. Sounds like it's addressing the reader but doesn't say anything. Real connection requires specificity.

**Good pattern — Questions that advance the reader's thinking:**
- "Did you know that only 3 percent of the world's population has actually created the life of their dreams?"
- "If you are 30 years of age and still not manifesting, how happy are you going to become over the next 10 years?"
- "Can you think of a time when your parent sat you down and focused on how to manifest consciously?" (Specific enough to trigger a real memory)

TJ's questions name something specific. They ask the reader to examine a real part of their experience, not just feel emotionally seen by vagueness.

## Pattern 4: Antithetical Parallelism (It's Not X, It's Y)

**What it is:** Framing things through negation before stating the positive, especially when stacked.

**Examples to avoid:**
- "It's not about money, it's about freedom."
- "You're not lost, you're found."
- Stacked negations: "It's not this. It's not that. It is the other thing."
- "It's not willpower. It's not discipline. It's alignment."

**Why it signals AI:** Creates a formulaic rhythm. Feels engineered. TJ's voice frames positively from the start without the negation setup.

**Good pattern — Reframe positively:**
- Instead of: "It's not about willpower, it's about alignment."
- Better: "Alignment is what actually changes behavior."
- Instead of: "You're not broken, you're just unaware."
- Better: "There's something specific you haven't learned yet."

When you need to address a reader's false belief, name it directly without the "it's not X" construction:
- Bad: "It's not about thinking the right way; it's about vibration."
- Good: "Vibration is what shifts your circumstances, not just your thoughts."

## Pattern 5: Rhyming or Rhythmic Patterns of Three

**What it is:** Three consecutive items with similar sound, structure, or syllable count that create a singsong or mechanical effect.

**Examples to avoid:**
- "Better, faster, stronger" (rhyming cadence)
- "Heal, reveal, appeal" (rhyming)
- "Three chances, three changes, three ranges" (artificial rhyme)
- Items with identical syllable count and stress: "Pick it up, lock it down, make it real" (four syllables each)

**Why it signals AI:** Creates rhythmic, singsong effects. Feels like the writing is chasing sound, not meaning. Real speech follows thought, not beat.

**Good pattern — Vary structure and length:**
- Mix short items with longer ones
- Vary word length and syllables
- Let content dictate structure
- Example: "You can shift this today. You can rebuild the pattern over weeks. Or you can spend the next decade wondering why nothing changes." (Varied length; varied rhythm)

## Pattern 6: Long Lists with Identical Parallel Structure

**What it is:** Multiple items in a series (usually 3+) that all follow the exact same grammatical form, creating mechanical feeling.

**Examples to avoid:**
- "It's costing you connection. It's stealing your time. It's damaging your relationships. It's diminishing your joy." (All "It's [verb]ing [object]")
- "You shut down when things get hard. You blame others when you're scared. You isolate when you feel alone." (All "You [verb] when [condition]")
- Repeated sentence structure throughout a piece creates robotic rhythm.

**Why it signals AI:** Mechanical. Formulaic. Lacks conversational flow. Suggests the writer is following a pattern, not thinking naturally.

**Good pattern — Vary grammatical structure within lists:**
- Short declarative sentence
- Longer explanatory sentence
- Question
- Fragment (if it serves the thought)
- Mix them in the same list
- Example: "Connection requires vulnerability. Most people aren't taught how to be vulnerable without falling apart. What happens when you finally learn? Everything changes."

---

## Pattern 7: Constructed Lists of Three at Multiple Points in the Copy

**What it is:** Using three-item lists as a rhetorical device multiple times throughout a single piece, creating a template-like feel.

**Examples to avoid:**
- Opening with "Three reasons why..." then later: "Three ways to know..." then later: "Three steps to take..."
- Each section breaks into exactly three points, creating mechanical pacing
- The structure feels like a formula rather than organic development

**Why it signals AI:** Creates templated, scaffold-like prose. Feels like filling slots rather than exploring ideas. Real writing develops ideas naturally; they may contain threes, but it's not a template.

**Good pattern:**
- Use natural list lengths. Sometimes two items fit. Sometimes five. Sometimes one long explanation without itemizing.
- If you do use a three-item list, the next list might be two items or a paragraph. Vary.
- Let the idea determine structure, not the structure determine ideas.

---

## Pattern 8: Instruction-Heavy or Listified Copy

**What it is:** Converting conversational teaching into numbered/bulleted instructions or steps when the original teaching was narrative.

**Examples to avoid:**
Converting TJ's narrative teaching: "The reason most people struggle is they've been told to visualize without understanding what visualization actually is. Visualization is alignment with a future state. Most people visualize outcomes without aligning their current behavior..."

Into: 
"1. Understand visualization
2. Align with future state
3. Update current behavior"

**Why it signals AI:** Loses TJ's conversational voice. Instructions and lists flatten the teaching into mechanical steps.

**Good pattern:**
- Keep narrative teaching in prose
- Use lists only when appropriate to the original teaching or when TJ himself uses lists
- Lists work for: ingredient lists, timeline points with dates, specific offer details, specific steps to take
- Lists do NOT work for: TJ's teaching concepts (keep conversational)

---

## When in Doubt

All of these patterns are variations on one principle:

**Avoid constructed rhetorical patterns that feel engineered rather than conversational.**

TJ's voice sounds like a person thinking and speaking—working through ideas—not like someone executing a rhetorical technique or following a template.

Return to this principle when making judgment calls on borderline cases.
